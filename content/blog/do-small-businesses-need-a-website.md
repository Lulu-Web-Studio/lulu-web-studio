Yes, most small businesses need a website.

You can technically start and run a business without one. Some businesses survive through referrals, walk-in traffic, social media, or a strong reputation in their community. But if a potential customer hears your name and looks you up, your website is often where they decide whether the business feels legitimate, current, and worth contacting.

That does not mean every business needs a large or expensive site. A plumber, restaurant, hair salon, consultant, and online store all need different things. For some, one clear page with services, hours, service area, and contact information is enough. For others, the website needs to generate leads, take bookings, sell products, or support local SEO.

The real question is not whether every small business needs the same kind of website. It is what job your website needs to do.

## Why do small businesses need a website?

A website gives your business an online home that you control.

Your Google Business Profile, Facebook page, Instagram account, online directory listings, and marketplace profiles can all help people find you. I recommend using the platforms that make sense for your customers. But those platforms decide how your business appears, who sees your posts, and what features you can use.

Your website gives you more control over your brand, information, and customer experience. It can show people what you do, who you help, what makes you different, and how to take the next step.

That matters for a few practical reasons.

### A website helps people trust the business

When someone is comparing two unfamiliar businesses, they look for signs that each one is real.

They may check reviews, photos, contact information, social media, and the website. If one business has a clear, professional site and the other has no site at all, the first business often feels easier to trust.

The website does not create trust by itself. A sloppy, outdated, or confusing site can have the opposite effect. But a good site gives you room to show real proof:

- Customer reviews
- Project photos
- Before-and-after examples
- Certifications or licenses
- Service details
- A real phone number and business email
- The areas you serve
- Clear policies and expectations

A website is part of your business's brand identity. It is your online face. The colors, photos, words, and layout should give customers the same feeling they would get from visiting your location or speaking with you.

### Customers can find answers without calling

Small-business owners answer the same questions all the time.

What are your hours? Do you serve my town? Do you take walk-ins? Can I book online? Do you offer this specific service? Where can I park? How long does the process take?

A useful website answers those questions at any hour. That saves the customer time and can reduce routine calls, messages, and emails for the business.

It also makes the business more accessible to people who cannot or do not want to make a phone call. Clear written information, contact forms, online booking, and text or email options give customers more ways to take action.

### A website can help you appear in local search

When people search for a nearby service, Google needs clear information about what the business does and where it operates.

A complete Google Business Profile is important, but a website gives search engines more context. You can create focused pages for individual services, locations, frequently asked questions, and useful topics your customers search for.

For example, a landscaper can have separate pages for lawn care, hardscaping, seasonal cleanup, and the towns they serve. A salon can explain each service, show pricing guidance, answer preparation questions, and link directly to booking.

A website does not guarantee a top Google position. Local competition, reviews, content quality, technical setup, links, and the age of the business all play a part. Still, it gives your [local SEO](/services/seo) work a much stronger foundation than a social profile alone.

### You control the message and customer path

Social media is useful for attention and conversation. Your website is where you can organize the full story.

You decide what appears first, which services get the most attention, what proof customers see, and what you want them to do next. That next step might be calling, requesting a quote, booking an appointment, visiting a location, or buying a product.

This control also matters when a platform changes its algorithm, removes a feature, suspends an account, or becomes less popular with your audience. Your domain and website give people a consistent way to find you.

### A website can support sales and daily operations

A small-business website can do more than display information. Depending on the business, it can:

- Collect quote requests
- Schedule appointments
- Sell products or gift cards
- Take deposits or payments
- Show menus or service lists
- Build an email list
- Share important updates
- Screen leads with a short form
- Provide customer instructions and FAQs

You do not need to add every possible feature. Start with the tasks that save time or make it easier for a good customer to buy.

These benefits line up with the main reasons covered in [Forbes' article on why every business needs a website](https://www.forbes.com/councils/theyec/2020/02/03/why-every-business-needs-a-website/): credibility, brand control, leads, search traffic, customer service, updates, and support for digital marketing.

## Can a business survive without a website?

Yes, a business can survive without a website.

A well-established local business may get enough work from repeat customers and referrals. A food truck may communicate through Instagram. A new freelancer may find all of their first clients through a professional network. A restaurant in a high-traffic location may depend heavily on walk-ins and delivery apps.

But surviving is a low bar.

Without a website, the business may lose customers who want to research before they call or visit. It may look less established than a competitor. It also has fewer opportunities to appear for searches related to its services.

I would look at customer behavior before deciding. Ask:

- Do people search online before buying this service?
- Do customers need to see examples, prices, menus, or reviews?
- Do they ask the same questions before contacting the business?
- Would online booking, ordering, or quote requests make buying easier?
- Are competitors getting found through Google?
- Does the business need a professional link for proposals, networking, or vendor applications?

If the answer to several of these is yes, relying only on referrals or social media is probably limiting the business.

## What can I use instead of a website?

If you are not ready for a website, start by claiming the online profiles your customers actually use.

Depending on the business, that may include:

- A Google Business Profile
- A Facebook business page
- An Instagram or TikTok account
- A LinkedIn company page
- A Yelp or industry directory profile
- An Etsy, Amazon, or other marketplace store
- A booking platform
- A delivery app profile
- An email newsletter

These options can work as a temporary starting point. They can also remain useful after the website launches.

The tradeoff is control. A social profile can limit how you present information. A marketplace can charge fees. A directory can display competitors beside your listing. Any outside platform can change its rules, reach, or pricing.

I usually recommend treating these profiles as paths that lead people toward the business, not as the only place the business exists online.

## Can a business not have a website?

There is no general rule that every business must have a website. Whether a specific business has legal online requirements depends on its location, industry, products, and services.

The practical issue is customer expectation. People often search for a business before they trust it with their time, money, property, health, or personal information. If they cannot find a reliable website, some will keep looking.

A business without a website should at least make sure its contact information, hours, location, and services are accurate across its main online profiles. Inconsistent or missing information makes the business harder to trust and harder to find.

## What does a small-business website actually need?

A basic site does not need ten pages, animations, or complicated features. It needs to answer the questions standing between the customer and the next step.

For many small businesses, that means:

- A clear explanation of what you do
- The types of customers you help
- Your location or service area
- Current hours and contact information
- A simple call to action
- Service or product details
- Real photos when possible
- Reviews, examples, or other proof
- An accessible, mobile-friendly layout
- A secure connection
- Fast, reliable loading
- A privacy policy if you collect personal information

Your domain can also support a business email address. An address using your own domain often looks more established than a personal email account.

The most important feature depends on the business. A restaurant needs an easy-to-read menu, hours, location, and reservation or ordering options. A plumber needs service areas, emergency availability, proof of experience, and a prominent phone number. A salon needs service information, photos, policies, and booking.

Build around what your customers are trying to accomplish.

## When is a DIY website enough?

A DIY website can be the right choice when you are starting out, have a limited budget, and only need a few straightforward pages.

Wix or Squarespace may be a good fit if you want something clean, manageable, and easy to update. Shopify can make sense when the main job is selling standard products online. A template is not automatically a bad decision.

The limits usually show up when the business needs a more specific brand identity, a larger SEO structure, unusual features, integrations, or a carefully planned sales path. Website builders work within their available layouts and features. That can be perfectly fine at first, but frustrating when the business outgrows them.

I would rather see a small business publish a clear, accurate starter site than delay for a year while planning a perfect custom build.

## When should you hire a professional?

Professional help makes sense when the website has a serious business goal and getting it wrong would cost more than the project.

Consider hiring someone when:

- You need the site to generate leads from search
- Your time is better spent serving customers or making sales
- Your current site makes the business look less professional
- You need custom booking, ecommerce, forms, or integrations
- You have multiple services or locations to organize
- You are running ads
- You need help with messaging, design, and content
- You have tried DIY and cannot get the result you want

The cost of a website depends on the amount of strategy, copywriting, design, development, content, ecommerce, integrations, and support involved. A one-page informational site and a custom online store should not cost the same because they are not the same job.

If you are comparing options, ask each provider to explain what is included, who owns the domain and accounts, what ongoing costs to expect, and how updates will work.

## What should you prepare before hiring a web designer?

You do not need to arrive with every word written or every image selected. You should have a clear business goal.

Before speaking with a designer or developer, gather:

- A list of your main services or products
- The locations you serve
- Your most common customer questions
- Examples of websites you like and dislike
- Reviews, project photos, certifications, and other proof
- The action you want visitors to take
- Any tools the site needs to connect to
- A realistic budget range
- Your preferred launch timing

Also think about the identity the website should communicate. Should the business feel warm and personal, polished and premium, bold and energetic, calm and reassuring, or practical and direct?

That answer helps a professional create the right first impression instead of choosing a style based on personal taste alone.

## How do you avoid hiring the wrong website company?

Be careful with anyone who promises a first-place Google ranking, recommends features without asking about the business, or cannot explain their process in plain language.

Ask practical questions:

- Can I see websites you have built?
- Who will own the domain, website, content, and accounts?
- Is copywriting included?
- Is mobile testing included?
- What SEO work is included?
- How will forms, analytics, and search tools be tested?
- What happens after launch?
- Are there recurring fees?
- Can I update the site myself?
- What happens if we stop working together?

Get the scope in writing. Make sure you know how many pages, revisions, features, and rounds of support are included.

A good provider should recommend the simplest site that can meet the goal. If a one-page builder site is enough, they should be comfortable saying so. If the business needs a more complete [custom website](/services/custom-website-development), they should be able to explain why without hiding behind technical language.

## So, do small businesses need a website?

Most do, but not every small business needs an expensive one.

Start with the customer. Give people a dependable place to understand the business, see proof, find answers, and take the next step. For a new business, that may be a single clear page. For a growing company, it may become a full marketing, booking, or sales system.

The website should fit the stage of the business and the way customers buy.
